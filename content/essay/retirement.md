I always expected to retire some day; when I was tired of working, when I had saved enough money, when I had plans for how to spend my time when I no longer had to earn a living wage. I’ve been writing software and managing software developers since my 20’s, and the field has shifted significantly with the growth of advanced machine learning (sometimes euphemistically and incorrectly called “artificial intelligence,” or “AI”). When my employer decided that I was no longer needed in July 2026, they fired me (euphemistically “let me go”).

Since I’m 65, I could not envision another company that would be willing to hire me. The tech industry is changing, and I have chosen to not keep up. While the fundamentals of software engineering remain the same, the actual mechanics of building software are different now, and, quite frankly, not as fun. Fundamentally, I am a creator, and I’m motivated by the challenges of the creative process. When I was in high school, I took the ASVAB (Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery) test, which told me, first, that I was unlikely to be a good soldier, sailor, or airman, and that, second, my motivations were the recognition of my work and the mental rewards of solving complex problems. In today’s software engineering realm, recognition comes to those who build the AI models or find innovative ways to use them, and not to the ones who use those tools to build websites or applications. Likewise, the complex problems to be solved are those involved in creating and managing the large language models (LLMs) themselves. 

To use an analogy, I used to be a sculptor, carving busts from granite. Today, sculptors create busts by submitting prompts to a robot that performs the carving based on scans of all prior busts that have been found). It is, admittedly, a much more efficient way of creating sculptures, but far less rewarding to the person (myself) actually responsible for producing the bust. Economically, it makes sense, and I understand it: anyone who wants a bust of their grandfather can now afford one, but that business is built on the creativity and skill of those prior sculptors who did it the hard way and whose work is now being repurposed (usually with no compensation at all) to do this on an industrial scale. Simply automating a process that used to require skill and creativity no longer interests me.

Note that I am not a Luddite who will push back against any modern technology. Like my father before me, who purchased both the first video game (Pong) and one of the earliest consumer video camcorders, I am an early adopter of new technologies. Software engineering has moved to a new phase, one that involves more engineering and less creativity, and I’m not motivated to participate. 

In the months since my firing, I’ve been able to get out with my camera more often. I’ve been practicing the guitar (must build up those calluses) and actually making progress on my reading goals. I am interacting with far fewer people than in the past (if you’re familiar with humanity, you will see this as both a good and a bad thing) and getting more sleep. I sometimes wonder about how my former team members are doing, and hoping that they’re happy and successful in whatever they do. 

If you’re ever near Seattle and want to chat, I’d be happy to buy you lunch.
